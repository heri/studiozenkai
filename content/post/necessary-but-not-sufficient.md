The progression of software engineers is like this:

* Junior. You have theoritical knowledge of software but most of it is obscure for you. The focus here is learning a framework, learning how development processes work, and delivering code that works. You are not able to fully develop applications but can do minor tasks
* Senior. You have launched a few applications and can do autonomously 95% of most tasks. You are not battle-tested ready yet, but can resolve most urgent issues. You are able to onboard junior developers.
* Staff Engineer or Lead Engineer. Like a compass, you are able to set a direction for the engineering team, and can navigate complex challenges, often involving product and other teams. You can tell horror stories where the prod database was lost, or where the team had to spent friday afternoon shipping a hotfix because of a rare but critical issue.
* Executive position. You contribute to key business milestones for the company. Most of your time is spent meeting, hiring, managing i.e maximize producitvity. And then there's odd monthly code contribution.

For Juniors and Seniors, the key to success is tech expertise. Common work issues is resolved by whoever has the fastest or most secure implementation. Numbers and hard facts are used. This is where you understand tech expertise is necessary.

This is not the case for staff engineers, the next step. In other industries such as civil engineering or mechnical engineering, you can rely on mathemetical formulas and sheer expertise. Software engineering model human processes. Human is complex and messy, and so is software. As a result, any leadership position in software requires talent in resolving human issues: what do people want, what do people care about, what issue or point did my team forgot about, how do I resolve the conflict between programmer A who doesn't agree with programmer B, how do we deliver this software before the marketing launch even if my top programmer is going on vacation. So a quick conclusion for this step is that tech expertise is not sufficient.

So it is critical for staff engineers to sit down and make plans. Can we list risks? Are there unknowns? What are our margins? If there unknowns, what is the B plan if the main plain fails? Do we want to allocate all the team's resources in the main project or do we want to hedge and have someone put time in a B plan? Experience can help answer a few of these. Often, answers actually comes from within the company. Stakeholders will tell what is best for them and point out threats. Even junior views are appreciated as their fresh minds or naivety can help re-evaluate the objective.

When mentoring other engineers, I sometimes use the metaphore of US Congress(wo)men and constituents. You go around, door to door, and try get the feedback of constituents to see what they all need. Another metaphore I use is of adventure games such as Mass Effect or Witcher 3. In these games, you have sub-quests. You navigate complex dialog trees, initating communication with different people, choosing trade-offs, even sometimes taking to cryptic NPCs for hints. You build relationships with NPCs (your crew), earning trust and alignement across departments and companies. You don't mind going through complex dialog trees and grinding for gear because you know it will allow you to finish the level and the game.

In a typical project, NPCs can be product managers, paying or free users, coworkers, or even far away teams like legal or marketing. Ask questions like Why? Why are we doing this? What do we want to get out of it? Why do you propose we do it this way? What are priorities?

If you ask the questions above, you will be able to get the team adopt your suggested solutions, whereas before other solutions will be chosen. You will be able to deliver a complex project, even when dates, constraints and requirements were fuzzy. 

When you are a staff engineer, can you get away with just being the best ever technically? If you have a pHd in machine learning or authored a published paper in Computer Science, yes you can just be "the best ever" and forget about having to find answers to complex issues. The majority of software engineers will have to go through the usual way, and be reminded that tech expertise is necessary but not sufficient.
