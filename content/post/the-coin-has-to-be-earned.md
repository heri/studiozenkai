When I was a kid in Madagascar, physical education was standing in rows with wooden rifles. Straight, left, right, up, down. Shout. Repeat. Real achievement was rare, and when it happened, it happened without ceremony.

<!--more-->

I have two kids now, born in Montreal, growing up in a world of abundance. Good schools, good food, devices, activities, options. Watching them, I kept thinking about two ways of raising high-achieving children, and how both seemed wrong to me.

## Two models, two problems

The first model is the East Asian drilling approach. In China it is called the sea of exercises tactic: students wade through an ocean of practice problems until every variation is memorized by repetition. Japan and Korea have their own versions of the same philosophy. It produces measurable results. East Asian students consistently lead global rankings in mathematics and science. But the mechanism is volume and pressure, not curiosity. The kid learns to endure, not to want.

The second model is what I see more of here in North America. No drilling, but also no real difficulty. Kids are offered an endless parade of activities, toys, and screens under the belief that passion will emerge on its own. Resilience is never built because it is never required. Every participation gets a ribbon. Every attempt gets a trophy.

I do not believe in either model.

## The third way

In the military, a challenge coin is a small medallion given to a service member who has done something that deserves recognition. You cannot buy one. You cannot be handed one without cause. If you present your coin and others cannot present theirs, they buy the drinks. The coin is proof. Proof that you were there, that you did the thing, that someone who witnessed it decided it was worth commemorating.

I built [My Challenge Coin](https://mychallengecoin.app) around that idea. A kid sets a challenge, attempts it, sometimes fails, tries again, and eventually does it. They take a photo. They write a short story about what happened. The coin appears on their wall. That coin is theirs. The story attached to it is true. And the kid who logged it knows, in a way that cannot be faked, that they did something real.

This is different from gamification. Gamification gives you points for showing up. Stars for streaks. Badges for consistency. Those systems work for habit formation, and I respect them. But they are not the same as earning something. I was careful to frame the app around legacy and memory, not points and levels. The language matters. Kids read it.

## Discipline and achievement are not the same thing

One thing I wanted to get right was the relationship between daily habits and real accomplishments. They feel related but they are not identical, and treating them as the same thing misleads kids.

You do not always summit the mountain today. But you can always make your bed.

In the app, Merit Points exist for daily discipline: made the bed, ate vegetables, practiced for an hour. They do not earn coins. They are not achievements. They are the foundation that makes achievements possible. A kid can log ten merit points in a week and still have an empty coin wall. That is honest. The coin wall only fills when something is actually done.

Above daily discipline sit challenges: discrete things that can be attempted, failed, and completed. Above challenges sit life milestones: a first black belt, a graduation, a competition podium. These are rare. They are meant to be rare. The pyramid only works if the peak stays hard to reach.

## The risk

I will name it plainly because I think about it.

A parent who downloads this app expecting a reward chart will be disappointed. The app does not hand things out. You have to put time and energy into it before it reap rewards. A kid who has not completed a challenge yet has an empty wall, and the empty wall is honest. Some kids will find that motivating. Others will not.

I have been running it with my two kids for six weeks. So far, they like it. I have shown it to a couple of other parents and they responded well. But I know this is the easiest possible sample: my own kids, parents who are already friends. The harder question is whether a kid who struggles with discouragement can sit with an empty wall long enough to earn their first coin.

I do not know the answer yet. I will find out as more families use it.

What I do know is that the empty wall is not a design flaw. It is the whole point. The coin means something because it was not given. That is the risk I decided to take, and I think it is the right one.
